Welcome

The iconic rolling hills of the Palouse, a landscape defined by agriculture. (Photo: Alan Sun)

Welcome to "The Unseen Roots," a project exploring the inseparable link between the Palus people and the Palouse Prairie. When you look at the Palouse, you see rolling hills of wheat. My project is about what we don't see—the history and the people this agricultural landscape replaced.

I will contend that the historical removal and displacement of the Palus people directly contributed to the extensive agricultural transformation of the landscape, and that modern efforts to restore the native prairie must acknowledge and incorporate Indigenous knowledge and land management practices to be truly effective.

A Displaced People

Before the arrival of Euro-American settlers, the Palouse was not a wild, untouched wilderness, but a carefully managed environment. The Palus people lived in semi-permanent villages along the Snake and Palouse rivers, migrating in "seasonal rounds" to fish, hunt, and gather vital "first foods" like camas bulbs. Camas, a lily-like plant with a nutritious bulb, was a staple of their diet. Harvesting it was an act of stewardship; digging aerated the soil, and smaller bulbs were replanted to ensure future harvests (NPS). This cyclical relationship ensured the health of the prairie ecosystem. The seasonal rounds were not merely an economic strategy but a cultural institution that maintained social cohesion and spiritual connection to the land. Families would gather at traditional fishing and harvesting sites, reinforcing kinship ties and passing down ecological knowledge to younger generations.

However, federal policies systematically dismantled this way of life. The Homestead Act of 1862 encouraged westward expansion by offering 160 acres of "public" land to settlers for a small fee, effectively incentivizing the theft of Indigenous territories. This was compounded by the Dawes Act of 1887, which sought to assimilate Native Americans by breaking up communal Tribal lands into small, individual plots (U.S. National Park Service). The "surplus" land was then sold to white homesteaders. These acts did more than just transfer land ownership; they imposed a foreign concept of private property onto a shared ecosystem, replacing the seasonal rounds of the Palus with the static grid of fences and farms (University of Washington). By forcing Indigenous families onto individual allotments, the government deliberately undermined the communal structures that had managed the landscape for millennia, paving the way for the monoculture agriculture that would follow.

An Altered Land

The consequences of this displacement were ecological as well as cultural. Today, less than 1% of the original Palouse Prairie remains, making it one of the most endangered ecosystems in the United States (Washington State University, "Hudson"). The conversion of deep-rooted native bunchgrasses to annual crops like wheat and lentils destabilized the soil. Native grasses have expansive root systems appearing several feet deep, which act as a net to hold the fine-grained loess soil in place. Wheat, by contrast, has a shallow root system that dies back every year. Without the perennial cover, the steep loess hills became incredibly vulnerable to wind and water erosion. The loess itself, formed by wind-blown dust over thousands of years, is highly susceptible to displacement once the protective vegetative skin is removed. Historical records show that in severe years, tons of topsoil could be lost from a single acre, permanently diminishing the land's fertility.

Organizations like the Palouse Conservation Environmental Institute (PCEI) are doing critical work to restore small patches of this native landscape. Their projects often focus on riparian restoration—planting native trees and shrubs along streams to stabilize banks, filter runoff, and cool water temperatures for salmon (Palouse Conservation Environmental Institute). Yet, ecological restoration that ignores the region's human history is incomplete. To be truly effective, these efforts must incorporate Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK).

TEK is not just "knowledge about the land"; it is an adaptive understanding acquired over thousands of years. For example, the Nez Perce tribe's use of controlled burns prevented the encroachment of shrubs and maintained the open prairie habitat essential for camas and other traditional foods. Modern restorationists are now beginning to adopt similar techniques, recognizing that the health of the land is inextricably linked to the practices of its original stewards.

The Story of the Land

This gallery summarizes the entire story: the land as it was, the process of transformation, the ecological result, and the path toward restoration.

The "Before": A rare remnant of native Palouse Prairie, like the Hudson Biological Reserve at WSU, which contains high-quality native vegetation (Washington State University, "Hudson").

The "Process": The Homestead Act encouraged settlers to "improve" the land, creating the iconic rolling farmlands we see today but erasing the native prairie ecosystem (University of Washington).

The "Result": Intensive farming on the region's steep slopes led to severe water and wind erosion, a massive ecological challenge (Washington State University, "Erosion").

The "Future": Modern restoration by groups like PCEI involves replanting native trees and shrubs to stabilize streambanks and restore habitat (Palouse Conservation Environmental Institute).

Restoration Guide: Learning from the Land

Restoring the Palouse isn't just about planting seeds; it's about understanding the ecosystem. Here is a guide inspired by both modern science and Indigenous knowledge.

Restoration in progress. (Source: PCEI)

  1. Know Your Soil: The Palouse has deep, loess soils. Restoration experts apply this by preventing erosion through "no-till" farming, which mimics the natural soil cover and keeps the structure intact (Huggins & Reganold). This method leaves crop residue on the surface, protecting the soil from the direct impact of rain and wind.
  2. Plant Natives: Use native bunchgrasses like Idaho Fescue and Bluebunch Wheatgrass. These deep-rooted plants stabilize the soil better than introduced crops. Their roots can extend six feet or more into the earth, accessing deep moisture and physically binding the soil particles together against runoff.
  3. Respect the Cycle: Learn from TEK. Harvests should be sustainable. If planting camas, ensure the soil is aerated and leave smaller bulbs to regenerate (NPS). This ensures that the population remains robust for future years, illustrating a reciprocal relationship with the plant rather than one of extraction.
  4. Water Wisdom: Restore riparian buffers (streamside vegetation) to cool the water for salmon and prevent runoff, a practice championed by groups like PCEI. Native shrubs provide shade that lowers water temperature—critical for fish survival—while their roots filter out agricultural pollutants before they reach the stream.

A Path Forward

The ecological state of the prairie and the human history of the Palus are not two separate issues; they are one. The landscape we see today was only possible after the removal of its original stewards. Therefore, we cannot restore one without the other. The goal is not just to return the plants, but to restore the relationship.
